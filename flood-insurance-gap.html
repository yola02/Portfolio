Where Do Flood Claims Fall Short? A Dashboard for a Real Client

A final project for DSC 502 (Data Visualization): an interactive Tableau dashboard built for an actuarial scientist, using FEMA flood insurance claims.

The Client and the Question

My client, Thérèse Ghanem, is an actuarial scientist. She had already analyzed FEMA's flood insurance claims in a technical way, but the results were hard for non-technical readers to follow. She needed an answer to one question: where, and by how much, are homeowners underinsured?

We defined "underinsured" in two ways. The main way is that the insurance payout was lower than the actual building damage. The second is that the coverage was lower than the value of the home. The test for success was simple: she can open a live link and, without any technical explanation, see how much of the building damage insurance did not pay in each state, then drill down by flood zone and year.

The Finished Visualization

Figure 1. The final dashboard, built in Tableau Public. Click a state to see its counties, choose a flood zone or a year range, and switch between the two measures. By default it shows the gap after the deductible, with major events hidden. Open it in a new tab.

Where the Data Came From

The data is FEMA's public NFIP Redacted Claims file from OpenFEMA (version 2, frozen as of June 1, 2026; FEMA has since released version 3). It has about 2.7 million claims and 73 columns, and each row is one claim. I kept single-family homes in Louisiana, Texas, Florida, New Jersey and New York from 1996 to 2024, which is 1,037,876 claims. About 802,000 of them have both a damage amount and a payout, and those are the claims the dashboard measures.

The full file is about 1 GB, so I used Python to turn it into a summary table of 17,156 rows, which keeps the dashboard fast. The data audit turned up two things that shaped the design. About 20% of the claims have no damage amount, and 84% of those were closed without payment, so the dashboard only measures paid claims and says so on screen. Before 1996 there were too few claims per year to trust.

How I Got There

I started with a data audit and an exploration of the claims. The chart that surprised me most showed Louisiana with 12.4% of building damage unpaid, but only 6.5% once the Katrina year was removed. That led to my working takeaway: most of what a typical claim leaves unpaid is the deductible, which is what the homeowner agrees to pay before insurance pays. For a typical claim, the median unpaid amount was $968 and the median deductible was $1,000. The large gaps come from a few catastrophes and from coastal flood zones.

I drew three sketches: a small time series for each state, a scrolling story, and a map dashboard. I showed them to Thérèse and presented them to my class for feedback before building the final version.

Two Design Decisions

1. I chose the map dashboard over the other two sketches, and opened it on the states. I chose the map because Thérèse preferred it, it was the only sketch that showed the gap geographically, and counties varied much more than states (from about 2% to 25% in my exploration). I decided against opening with a story. When I presented the sketches in class, most people, like Thérèse, preferred opening directly on the state map and clicking into counties, while some said a short story introduction would not hurt. To serve both, I put the state map, the county map and the flood zone grid side by side on one screen, with a short "How to read this dashboard" guide at the top. First-time viewers get the context they need without a separate story. I also left out a line chart over time that came up in the feedback, because the flood zone by year grid already shows change over time.

2. I made ordinary years, after the deductible, the default view. I decided against showing every year by default. A few catastrophe years hold between 81% and 97% of each state's damage dollars, so they change the picture a lot: after the deductible, Louisiana is 3.1% unpaid in ordinary years but 10.8% with the major events included. Thérèse wanted the states to be comparable, so the events are hidden at first, and a toggle brings them back.

Data: FEMA OpenFEMA, NFIP Redacted Claims v2 (dataset record on Data.gov). Definitions confirmed by the client. Built with Python (pandas) for the data preparation, Tableau Public for the dashboard, and Claude for code suggestions and feedback on drafts.
